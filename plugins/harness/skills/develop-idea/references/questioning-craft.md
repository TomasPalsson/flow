# Questioning Craft — how to ask so the idea grows

The gap between a developmental questioner and an interrogator is **discipline, not vocabulary**. Amateurs ask too many questions, ask "why" when they should ask "what," and confuse performing curiosity with practicing it. Experts ask one good question, go quiet, listen, and let *that answer* pick the next one. And they don't only ask — they alternate asking and asserting.

## Core techniques

**Single-thread follow** — when the last answer holds something interesting, underspecified, or contradictory, follow *that thread* and abandon your list.
- "You said 'nobody else is doing this' — what makes you confident?"
- "You mentioned X almost in passing. That seems like the real thing — say more?"
- "Go back to what you said two minutes ago — what did you mean exactly?"

**Steelman before the challenge** — articulate the strongest version (stronger than they said it), *then* introduce tension.
- "The strongest version of this is [X] — is that fair? Because if it's true, what about [tension]?"
- People shut down when attacked; steelmanning earns permission to probe. If you can't steelman it, you haven't understood it.

**"What" and "how" instead of "why"** — "why" triggers self-justification and defensiveness; "what/how" invite exploration.
- "Why will this work?" → "What makes you think this will work?"
- "Why is this different?" → "What makes this different, in your mind?"
- "Why would someone pay?" → "What would have to be true for someone to pay?"

**Productive aporia** — guide them to realize they don't know something they thought they knew, and *hold* them there long enough for real thinking. Don't rush to resolve confusion.
- "Let's sit with that — I'm not sure either of us knows the answer yet."
- "Earlier you said Y; now X. Not catching you out — genuinely curious how you hold both."

**The reflecting move** — mirror back with a slight shift in framing; the small distortion makes them clarify what they actually meant.
- "So this is fundamentally a distribution problem, not a product problem." (Wait.)
- "It sounds like you're not committed to X — you're committed to Y, and X was just the first path you saw."

**Surface the assumption** — name an embedded assumption to put it on the table, not to attack it.
- "That assumes [X]. Solid, or worth testing?"
- "You're treating [X] as fixed. What if it's not?"

**Expansion prompt** — when an idea is undersized, push for a *bigger* version, not more detail.
- "What's the 10x version?" · "If it absolutely worked, what does it look like in five years?" · "Who else has this problem that you haven't thought about?"

**The commitment move** — after enough exploration, stop opening threads; force a decision.
- "Given all that — what's the one thing you'd do differently now?" · "If you had to put a stake in the ground, what's the core bet?"

**Silence** — after a good question, stop talking. Don't fill it, don't soften it. Most of the valuable material — the part they didn't plan to say — comes *after* the pause. Discomfort is information.

**Name the meta-level** — when the *pattern* of the conversation is revealing, name it.
- "Every time we get near [X], you pull back. What's there?"
- "We've talked 20 minutes and you haven't mentioned [obvious stakeholder]. Why?"

## The assert-vs-ask decision

Good development is NOT all questions. Alternate. The question is which move serves their thinking *now*.

**Assert when:** they're stuck in a loop and need a frame to react against · the problem is obvious and making them "discover" it is condescending · you've earned it through listening · time is short and they need a concrete rec · they explicitly ask what you think (withholding is just performance).

**Ask when:** you don't yet understand what they mean (your assertion would be built on a misread) · they need to *discover* it to own it · multiple directions are plausible and you don't know which fits · your assertion might foreclose a line they need to explore.

**The rule:** assert to give them something to *react against*, never to foreclose. The hybrid that often beats a pure question: **"Here's what I think — tell me where I'm wrong."**

## The three question banks

**Expand** *(remove constraints — grow the idea outward)*
- "What's the biggest version you could imagine?" · "Who else has this problem outside your target market?" · "If the first version worked perfectly, what would you build next?" · "What's the adjacent problem this solves that you haven't named?" · "Who's done this in a different domain — what can you take from them?"

**Probe** *(add specificity — deepen what's there)*
- "What do you mean by [their word]?" · "How do you know that?" · "When you say X, what does that look like in practice?" · "What's an example?" · "Where does that number come from?"

**Challenge** *(introduce counter-pressure — without attacking)*
- "What's the strongest argument against what you just said?" · "What would a smart skeptic say?" · "What's the version where this fails, and how likely is it?" · "What have you heard from people who don't agree?" · "What would change your mind?"

**The functional difference:** expansion removes constraints, probing adds specificity, challenge adds counter-pressure. **Rough ratio: ~3 expansion/probe moves per 1 challenge while the idea is young.** Challenge without expansion is just criticism.

## Reading the level

**Push harder when:** they're defensive without engaging substance · agreeing too easily (compliance, not thinking) · confidence exceeds evidence · stakes are high and they're not feeling them.

**Back off when:** they go quiet in an overwhelmed (not thinking) way · they defend the same point repeatedly (they don't feel heard — go back to reflecting) · the idea is too early for challenge · you've asked three challenges in a row (it's an interrogation now).

## Bad-question anti-patterns
- **Question-list dump** → ask one, wait, follow the answer.
- **Leading question** ("Don't you think this is really B2B?") → "Who do you see as the primary customer?"
- **Why-interrogation** → swap to "what" / "how".
- **Fake Socratic** (steering to a conclusion you've pre-picked) → just assert it, then ask what they think.
- **Not listening** → repeat back one thing from their answer before the next question.
- **Over-challenging an immature idea** → match challenge to stage.
- **Double question** ("is it the product, or distribution, or timing?") → ask the single question, let them pick what matters to them.
- **Leaving without resolution** → close with "what will you actually do differently?"
